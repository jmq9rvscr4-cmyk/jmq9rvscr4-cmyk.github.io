Everyone Wants a Village

Community is not something we find waiting for us; it is something we create when we decide another person’s problem is worth a little of our time.

I recently found myself charging my car at a charging station in downtown Los Angeles when I noticed a woman nearby who seemed distressed.

The driver was stranded in downtown Los Angeles, standing beside an electric vehicle neither of us knew how to fix. I could have offered an apology, wished her luck, and kept walking. Instead, I stayed.

There was no dramatic rescue. No sudden insight. We made a call to roadside assistance, sorted through a confusing problem, and waited. The most useful thing I did was not solve everything. It was refuse to leave the person alone with it.

That small inconvenience clarified something I had been thinking about for a long time: we often speak of community as though it is a place we hope to belong to, but a village is built by people willing to become useful to one another. Everyone wants the comfort of a village. Fewer people volunteer for the inconvenience of being a villager.

Help is usually inconvenient

In the version of helping that we imagine, you have exactly what the person needs. You know what to do, solve the problem, and leave certain that you made a difference.

Real help is messier. You may not know the answer. It may take longer than you expected. The person may be frightened, embarrassed, or understandably suspicious of a stranger who has suddenly inserted himself into the situation.

You do not need to be an expert to be useful. Sometimes you stay and figure things out together. You make the call someone is too overwhelmed to make. You narrow a confusing set of options down to one manageable next step.

I couldn’t solve every problem that day. I could make sure another person didn’t have to face it alone.

Competence is a form of care

Compassion is often described as a feeling. I tend to experience it more practically. When someone needs help, I start asking questions. What is the immediate risk? What resources are available? What does this person need right now?

My background in animal care shaped that instinct. A vulnerable patient does not benefit from panic or grand gestures. You pay attention, communicate clearly, and act on what you know.

The same is true when the problem is mechanical, administrative, or simply human. Caring matters, but it is what you do with that care that helps someone move forward.

The mathematics of opting out

Any one person can reasonably decide that a stranger’s problem is not theirs. Most of us are busy. Sometimes stopping is unsafe, and good judgment includes knowing when not to get involved.

But if everyone makes the individually reasonable choice to keep moving, we create a world in which nobody stops.

Then we wonder why the world feels so lonely.

Community does not require giving everyone unlimited access to us or abandoning our boundaries. It asks for some small, proportionate responsibility when we are in a position to help. That might mean giving someone a little of our time, sharing what we know, or simply refusing to treat another person’s distress as background scenery.

Build the thing you wish existed

“Be the change you want to see” has been repeated so often that it can sound decorative. The demand behind it is still real. If I want to live in a world where someone would stop for me, I have to help make stopping normal.

A village is built through ordinary choices: the phone call answered, the ride given, the confusing form explained, the person who stayed ten minutes longer than required.

We do not become villagers by declaring that community matters. We become villagers when someone else’s bad day briefly becomes our business and we choose to be useful.
