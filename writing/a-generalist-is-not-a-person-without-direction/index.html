A Generalist Is Not a Person Without Direction

My interests make more sense when they’re organized by the question underneath them.

On paper, my interests do not form an especially tidy line. Veterinary medicine. Technology. Cars. Design. Government. Cooking. The small, strange mechanics of everyday life. I can become intensely interested in almost anything once I find the part that does not quite make sense.

For a long time, I treated that range as evidence that I had failed to choose a direction. The usual advice is to specialize: pick one thing, become legible, and let that single identity explain you. There is practical wisdom in developing depth. But there is also a difference between having no direction and having a direction that runs underneath several subjects.

My subjects change. The question usually does not: How does this work, where is it failing, and can I make it better?

The pattern beneath the subjects

Veterinary medicine taught me to pay attention when details have consequences. A patient cannot explain what hurts, a family may be overwhelmed, and several people may each hold only one piece of the picture. The work requires observation, translation, prioritization, and the willingness to notice when a confident answer is not supported by the evidence.

Those habits followed me outside the hospital. When a device behaves strangely, I want the logs. When an insurance decision contradicts its own underlying numbers, I want the timeline. When a process is confusing, I want to know whether the problem is genuinely complex or merely organized badly.

The topics look unrelated if I sort them by industry. If I sort them by method, they are remarkably consistent.

Curiosity is useful when it produces structure

Curiosity by itself can become endless collection: another tab, another theory, another unfinished path. The version I value has an output. It converts confusion into a sequence someone can act on.

  1. Define what is actually happening, not what the label says is happening.
  2. Separate the known facts from assumptions that have quietly hardened into facts.
  3. Find the constraint that changes the decision.
  4. Explain the reasoning clearly enough that another person can challenge it.

That last step matters. A conclusion without visible reasoning can sound like certainty for its own sake. Showing the path makes the idea testable. It also makes collaboration possible.

Depth and range are not enemies

Being a generalist does not excuse shallow work. In fact, moving between fields makes rigor more important. You cannot rely on familiarity to hide a weak assumption. You have to learn the vocabulary, understand the stakes, and know when the responsible answer is “I don’t know yet.”

Range becomes valuable when it allows one field to illuminate another. Clinical triage can teach you how to prioritize a technical failure. Good interface design can teach you why an administrative process keeps producing mistakes. A dispute can teach you that documentation is not clerical overhead; it is a model of reality that other people can inspect.

I still believe in choosing anchor skills and doing the unglamorous work required to improve them. I just no longer believe that every other interest is a distraction from the real path.

A direction can be a way of moving

I am not trying to become a person who knows everything. I am trying to become someone who can enter an unfamiliar problem without being intimidated by the fact that it is unfamiliar.

My direction is not a single subject. It is a way of paying attention: learn the system, locate the mismatch, ask better questions, and leave the situation more understandable than I found it.
